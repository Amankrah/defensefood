2. Commodity Dependency Models

This section denes the mathematical models for quantifying how dependent a destination coun-
try (attention/alert country) is on a specic origin country for a specic commodity. This is the
structural vulnerability layerit captures the conditions that make fraud economically viable
and dicult to resist.

2.1 Domestic Supply Balance

The foundation of all dependency analysis is the commodity supply balance equation.

Denition 2.1 (Domestic . Supply) For commodity c in country i at time t:

DS(c, i, t) = P (c, i, t) + M (c, i, ·, t) - X(c, i, ·, t) + S(c, i, t)            (1)

where S(c, i, t) is the change in stocks (draw-down positive, build-up negative).

    When stock change data is unavailable (common at commodity level), set S = 0 to obtain

apparent domestic supply:

DS(c, i, t) = P (c, i, t) + M (c, i, ·, t) - X(c, i, ·, t)                              (2)

       This is the standard FAO simplication used in Food Balance Sheets. Where FAOSTAT
provides pre-computed balance data, use it directly. Where it does not, compute from Comtrade
(imports, exports) and FAOSTAT/Eurostat (production).

2.2 Import Dependency Ratio (IDR)

Denition 2.2 . (Import Dependency Ratio) The share of country i's total domestic

supply of commodity c that comes from imports:

IDR(c, i, t) = DS(c, i, t) = P (c, i, t) + M (c, i, ·, t) - X(c, i, ·, t)          (3)

Property 2.1 (IDR . Interpretation)

 IDR = 0: Fully self-sucient. All supply is domestic production.

 IDR = 1: Fully import-dependent. No domestic production (net of re-exports).

 0 < IDR < 1: Partial dependency. Higher values = greater import reliance.

Property 2.3 (HHI . Properties)

  HHI = 1/n for n equal suppliers: perfectly diversied.
  HHI = 1: single-supplier monopoly.
  HHI > 0.25: highly concentrated (standard antitrust threshold). A small number of origins

    The HHI captures systemic vulnerability beyond what a single-corridor BDI shows. Even if

no single origin dominates, high HHI means the market is oligopolisticloss of any supplier has

amplier The (1 + HHI) term serves as an  : when the overall import market is concentrated,

5.3 Mirror Trade Discrepancy

Trade data is reported by both importer and exporter. Discrepancies may indicate fraud.

Denition 5.3 (Mirror Trade . Discrepancy)

5.4 Trade Network Concentration Shift

Monitor structural changes in import concentration over time:

HHI(c, i, t) = HHI(c, i, t) - HHI(c, i, t-1)                             (28)

warranting investigation.

6.1 Exposure Network as a Directed Graph

              wtrade(j, i, c, t) = M (c, i, j, t)                                       (30)

6.2 Origin Risk Propagation Score (ORPS)

For an origin country j: how broadly does its food fraud risk propagate across the EU?

Denition 6.2 (Origin Risk Propagation . Score)

ORPS(j, c, t) = BDI(c, i, j, t) · HIS(c, i, j, t) · PCC(c, i, t)                        (33)

              iN

       This sums, across all EU destination countries, the product of bilateral dependency, hazard

7. Composite Vulnerability Scoring

7.1 Normalisation

Each model output operates on a dierent scale. Before combination, they must be normalised.
Three defensible approaches:

7.1.1 Option A: Min-Max Normalisation

                   xnorm               =    x - xmin                     (36)
                                          xmax - xmin

Maps to [0, 1]. Simple but sensitive to outliers. xmin and xmax are computed over all corridors.

7.1.2 Option B: Percentile Rank Normalisation

                   xnorm = percentile_rank(x) across all corridors       (37)

    Maps to [0, 1]. Robust to outliers. Interpretation: this corridor is in the 85th percentile for

this metric. More stable when underlying distributions are skewed (which they will bemost
corridors are low-risk).

7.1.3 Option C: Log-Transform then Percentile

                   xnorm = percentile_rank ln(1 + x)                     (38)

       Appropriate for highly skewed metrics (notication counts, trade volumes) where a small
number of corridors dominate.

Recommendation: Option B (percentile rank) for most metrics. Option C (log-

percentile) for HIS due to exponential distribution of notication counts. Validate with
your data once a full corridor dataset is available.

8. What This Framework Does Not Yet Cover

The following are areas where the mathematical modelling requires input that has not been
specied:

 1. Price data source and granularity. The unit value analysis (5.1) uses Comtrade

         value/volume as a price proxy. This is crude. If actual import price indices (Eurostat)
         or wholesale market prices are available, the models become signicantly more precise.
         What price data do you have or plan to acquire?

 2. HS code mapping methodology. The entire framework depends on reliably matching

     RASFF commodity descriptions to HS codes for Comtrade matching. The Commodity_Code

         eld in your data is sometimes populated, sometimes not. What is the plan for missing
         values? This is a make-or-break data engineering problem.

 3. Temporal granularity. The models work at annual or monthly resolution. RASFF data

         is daily; Comtrade is annual/monthly; FAOSTAT is annual with 12 year lag. What is the
         target update cycle?

 4. Validation data. Do you have access to conrmed fraud cases (from FFN, OLAF, or

         Member State authorities) to validate model outputs? Without validation, the scoring
         system is a hypothesis, not a proven model.

 5. Scope of commodities. Your sample data covers cereals, feed materials, and bivalve

         molluscs. Dierent commodity types may require dierent parameterisations (e.g., the

      decay parameter  should be faster for perishable goods than for shelf-stable goods).
